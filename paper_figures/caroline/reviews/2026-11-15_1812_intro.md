### 2. Reviewer-level assessment

**The job.** The intro has to make three points, each backed by evidence. First, simulated Spectre defenses cannot be security-validated by running programs. Second, the only prior tester, AMuLeT, tests random programs against contracts that do not match each defense's guarantee. Third, SimSpect's three parts fix both problems, and the bug and efficiency results prove it. The arc is there, but the text undercuts it: the novelty claim contradicts itself, a prior bug is misdescribed so that it collides with our headline new bug, and several numbers are wrong or unsupported. Our own timing data also contradicts the efficiency claim.

**Weaknesses (as a hostile PC member would write them).**

- W1: "The paper says AMuLeT is 'the state-of-the-art approach for testing whether simulated Spectre defenses uphold their advertised security guarantees' (§1, ¶5), then claims to be 'the first … approach for validating … against their security guarantees'. The only argument for the difference is the unsupported claim that 'no instruction-level contract can capture such a guarantee exactly'. That claim ignores that hardware–software contracts are parametric and that AMuLeT's adversary model already restricts which channels are observed."
- W2: "Every result depends on hand-written specifications (§3, 'We write each clause by hand from the defense's paper'). The intro never says how a specification is validated. A wrong specification produces missed or spurious bugs, which is exactly the failure the paper blames on AMuLeT's contracts."
- W3: "The efficiency headline counts tests, not time. By the paper's own §5.4, SimSpect is about 7× slower than AMuLeT in wall-clock time on the rarest known bug and no faster on A-UV3. The '18×' minimum rests on a single A-UV5 detection, with a 95% interval of about 5–46×."
- W4: "Several of the six 'new bugs' hold only under non-default conditions: commitToIEWDelay > 1 for (6), tests forced through FP registers for (1), generated direct jumps for (5), and for (3) a swept resolution delay plus a gem5 x86 `mov` quirk. The harness also forces predictions and delays resolution. The intro discloses the condition only for (6)."
- W5: "The completeness claims ('enumerates every leakage backbone', 'instruction bound of six') do not hold for SPT, whose enumeration is capped. 'We specify eight defenses' has no support anywhere in the paper."
- W6: "Fig. 1 contains factual errors: CT-SEQ is labeled speculative non-interference, and the STT example specification contradicts §3.3. The intro also misdescribes prior bugs: it calls Protean's squash-signal bug a 'race' and places AMuLeT's five in-scope bugs in 'four' defenses."

**Questions for the rebuttal.**

- Q1: Which of the six new bugs could AMuLeT's contracts and adversary model not detect in principle, and which did it simply not hit? (Results says AMuLeT's own SpecLFB campaigns trigger (5) in 47 of 248 violations, results.tex:65.)
- Q2: How do you check a hand-written specification against the defense's paper, and what happens to your conclusions when a specification is wrong?
- Q3: Why are "tests" comparable units, when an AMuLeT test case is one program × one input and a SimSpect test is one program run once? What is wall-clock time to detection?
- Q4: Can forced mispredictions and stalled resolution create speculation that no real predictor produces? If so, are the bugs still bugs?
- Q5: Is the visibility-point/squash race (6) distinct from the squash-signal bug Protean fixed in STT/SPT/SPT-SB?
- Q6: Why specify NDA and SpecShield if you don't evaluate them? Why not evaluate the NDA and DoM schemes in the ReCon tree you already run?
- Q7: Is (3) a ReCon defect if generated tests reach it only because gem5's x86 `mov` reads its destination register?
- Q8: How much of the 18× comes from targeting, and how much from AMuLeT running several inputs per program?
